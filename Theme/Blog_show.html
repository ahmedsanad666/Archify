The Tactile Residence: Materiality as Narrative

By Elias Thorne October 24, 2024 8 min read

In the realm of contemporary residential architecture, there is a profound shift away from the sterile, ultra-polished surfaces of early 2000s minimalism. We are witnessing a return to materiality—a deep, visceral craving for spaces that speak to our sense of touch. The 'Tactile Residence' project embodies this philosophy, standing as a testament to the idea that a home is not just a visual composition, but a physical experience.

The Anatomy of Texture

When we embarked on this project, the immediate environment dictated a robust response. Located on a rugged, windswept coastline, the building needed to feel grounded, almost as if it had been excavated from the earth rather than placed upon it. We turned to a palette of raw, expressive materials: board-formed concrete, charred timber (Shou Sugi Ban), and patinated brass.

"Materiality is not just the finish; it is the dialogue between the hand and the space. A room should feel as intentional in the dark as it does in the daylight."

The board-formed concrete, used extensively in the primary living volumes, retains the memory of the timber formwork. It offers a rough, heavily shadowed texture that changes dramatically as the sun tracks across the sky. By night, when illuminated by our carefully calibrated, low-level warm lighting scheme, these walls take on a soft, almost monolithic warmth, absorbing light rather than reflecting it.

A Symphony of Shadows

We approached the fenestration not merely as a means to capture views, but as instruments for orchestrating light and shadow. Deep reveals and brise-soleil crafted from the same charred timber as the cladding modulate the intense coastal light, casting slow-moving geometric patterns across the interior surfaces throughout the day.

This manipulation of light ensures that the tactile qualities of the home are constantly redefined. It is an architecture that demands to be experienced slowly, revealing its complexities only to those who take the time to look, and more importantly, to feel.
